Is delivery imminent? — 3 checks

LookCrowning or bulging perineum. If you can see head, you are delivering here.
Listen"I need to push" / "I have to have a bowel movement" = fetal head on rectum.
FeelContractions q2–3 min, >60 sec, involuntary bearing down.
No digital exam until you have excluded previa in anyone with third-trimester bleeding. Bedside ultrasound first: fetal presentation, heart rate, placental location, number of fetuses.

The delivery

Read this top to bottom. Times are from delivery of the baby.

force you apply correct hand position ✕never Diagrams are schematic. They show direction of force and where your hands go, not anatomy.

Set up

2 min, in parallel
  • Mother: two large-bore IVs, monitor, type & screen or crossmatch, CBC, oxytocin drawn up and at the bedside before you need it.
  • Position: lithotomy with the bed broken down, or left lateral if she cannot tolerate supine. Sterile drape, gown, face shield — this is a messy procedure.
  • Tray: two clamps, scissors, bulb suction, cord clamp, warm dry towels ×4, sterile gloves.
  • Baby: radiant warmer preheated, hat, neonatal bag-mask with correct-size mask, suction, pulse oximeter, towels. Food-grade plastic wrap or bag if <32 weeks — do not dry preterm skin, wrap it wet.
  • Dating: fundal height in cm above the symphysis ≈ gestational age in weeks from 20–36. At the umbilicus ≈ 20 weeks.
Ask fastPrenatal care? Multiples? Prior cesarean? Known previa? Group B strep? Drug use? These change your plan and you will not get another chance to ask.

The head

Slow is the whole job
  • Let her push with contractions. Your hands are there to slow the head, not pull it.
  • Flat hand on the occiput applying gentle counter-pressure. An explosive head is what tears the perineum and shears intracranial vessels.
  • Support the perineum with a towel-covered hand; encourage panting between contractions to ease the head out between pushes.
  • Suction the mouth then nose only if there is visible obstruction. Routine bulb suction is no longer recommended and can cause reflex bradycardia.
  • Sweep for a nuchal cord as soon as the head is out — run a finger around the neck.
    • Loose → slip it over the head.
    • Tight → somersault maneuver: hold the head flexed against the thigh and let the body somersault out while the cord stays intact.
    • Only if truly unreducible → double clamp and cut. The clock on oxygen delivery starts the second you cut, so the body must come now.

The shoulders

Down, then up
  • Wait for restitution — the head turns on its own to face a thigh. Do not force it.
  • Anterior shoulder: hands on the parietal bones, gentle traction toward the floor.
  • Posterior shoulder: then lift gently toward the ceiling.
  • The body follows fast and is extremely slippery. Support the head and neck with one hand, hook the other under the axilla or grip the ankles. Keep the baby at or slightly below the level of the perineum.
  • Say the time of delivery out loud so someone documents it.
Turtle signThe head delivers, then retracts against the perineum and the shoulders do not follow. That is shoulder dystocia — jump to the card below and start the clock.

The baby

+0 to 60 sec
  • Warm, dry, stimulate, position the airway. Vigorous term baby → straight to the mother's chest, skin to skin, blanket and hat over both.
  • Three questions: Term? Good tone? Breathing or crying? All yes → routine care with the mother. Any no → warmer.
  • Apneic, gasping, or HR <100 → PPV within the first 60 seconds, rate 40–60/min. Start at 21% O₂ if ≥35 weeks, 21–30% if less.
  • Not improving on PPV → run MR SOPA: Mask reseat, Reposition head, Suction, Open mouth, Pressure up, Airway alternative.
  • HR <60 after 30 sec of effective ventilation → compressions 3:1 with breaths (90 compressions + 30 breaths per minute), go to 100% O₂, get umbilical venous access.
  • Check a glucose. Apgar at 1 and 5 minutes, and every 5 minutes to 20 if the 5-minute score is <7.

The cord

+30 to 60 sec
  • Delay clamping 30–60 seconds in any vigorous baby, term or preterm. Keep the baby warm and at the level of the perineum while you wait.
  • Clamp at roughly 2 cm and 4 cm from the abdominal wall, cut between with sterile scissors.
  • Collect cord blood for type and Coombs if the mother is Rh-negative or blood type is unknown.
Clamp now insteadBaby needs immediate resuscitation, or there is abruption, previa, cord avulsion, or monochorionic twins.

The placenta

5 to 30 min
  • Give oxytocin once the baby is out — 10 units IM, or 20–40 units in 1 L crystalloid run fast.
  • Three signs of separation: a gush of blood, the cord lengthening, the uterus rising and becoming firm and globular.
  • Only then, gentle controlled cord traction with your other hand pushing the uterus up and back above the symphysis (Brandt-Andrews). Counter-pressure is what prevents inversion.
  • Massage the fundus until firm. Inspect the placenta and membranes for completeness — a missing cotyledon means retained tissue and ongoing bleeding.
  • Inspect the perineum, vagina and cervix in good light. First and second degree tears you can repair; third or fourth degree involves the anal sphincter or rectal mucosa and goes to OB.
Do not pullTraction on a placenta that has not separated causes cord avulsion or uterine inversion. If it has not delivered by 30 minutes, that is retained placenta — call OB, do not escalate your pulling.

Hard stops

The small set of things that convert a manageable delivery into a catastrophe.

Never

  • Fundal pressure in shoulder dystocia. It impacts the shoulder further and ruptures uteri. Suprapubic only.
  • Digital exam with third-trimester bleeding until previa is excluded.
  • Cord traction before signs of placental separation.
  • Hard traction on the fetal head — brachial plexus injury is a traction injury.
  • Pull on a breech. Hands off until the scapulae are visible.
  • Methylergonovine in hypertension or preeclampsia.
  • Carboprost in asthma.
  • Uterotonics before replacing an inverted uterus.

When it goes wrong

These do not happen on a schedule. Note the time out loud when any of them starts.

Shoulder dystocia

Turtle sign — head delivers then retracts, shoulders do not follow with routine traction.

  1. Help. Call it out, note the time, get OB and peds in the room. Drop the head of the bed flat.
  2. Legs — McRoberts. Two assistants hyperflex the hips onto the abdomen. This alone resolves the majority.
  3. Suprapubic pressure directed downward and laterally onto the anterior shoulder to adduct it. Not fundal.
  4. Enter — rotational maneuvers: Rubin II (push the anterior shoulder toward the fetal chest), Woods screw, then reverse Woods.
  5. Remove the posterior arm. Sweep it across the chest and out. Highly effective when rotation fails.
  6. Roll onto all fours (Gaskin) and re-attempt.
  • Last resort: deliberate clavicular fracture, Zavanelli replacement with cesarean, symphysiotomy.
  • Consider episiotomy to make room for your hand — it does not relieve the bony impaction itself.

Cord prolapse

Palpable pulsating cord in the vagina, or sudden profound fetal bradycardia after rupture of membranes.

  1. Gloved hand into the vagina and elevate the presenting part off the cord. Do not remove it — you stay there, hand in place, all the way to the operating room.
  2. Knee-chest position or steep Trendelenburg.
  3. Do not attempt to reduce the cord and handle it as little as possible; manipulation causes vasospasm.
  4. Alternative if you must free your hand: fill the bladder with 500–700 mL saline via Foley, then clamp.
  5. Terbutaline 0.25 mg SC for tocolysis.
  6. Emergent cesarean. This is a surgical problem, not a delivery problem.

Breech

Buttocks or feet presenting. Wants an OR — but if it is already delivering, it is delivering.

  1. Hands off the breech. Let it deliver spontaneously to the umbilicus. Traction extends the arms and the head and causes entrapment.
  2. Keep the fetal back anterior. Wrap the body in a towel for grip, support only.
  3. Scapulae visible → sweep each arm down across the chest. Rotate 180° each way (Løvset) if an arm is trapped above the head.
  4. Head — Mauriceau-Smellie-Veit. Body straddles your forearm, index and middle fingers on the maxilla to flex the head, other hand on the shoulders with a finger on the occiput, gentle downward traction plus an assistant applying suprapubic pressure.
  • Highest risks: head entrapment, especially preterm where the body fits through an incompletely dilated cervix; and cord prolapse.

Postpartum hemorrhage

>1000 mL, or any bleeding with hemodynamic change. Tone, trauma, tissue, thrombin — atony is 70–80%.

  1. Bimanual massage — fist in the anterior fornix, other hand compressing the fundus from above. Start this while people fetch drugs.
  2. Oxytocin, then add a second agent from the dose table. Do not wait to see if the first one works alone.
  3. TXA 1 g IV over 10 min, within 3 hours of onset. Repeat once if still bleeding at 30 min.
  4. Two large-bore IVs, crossmatch, activate massive transfusion early.
  5. Look for what massage will not fix: retained placenta, cervical or vaginal laceration, vulvar hematoma, uterine inversion, rupture.
  6. Tamponade: Bakri balloon, or a Foley with 60–80 mL if that is what you have. Aortic compression buys time to the OR.

Uterine inversion

Fundus not palpable, a mass at the introitus, hemorrhage with shock out of proportion to blood loss.

  1. Stop all uterotonics.
  2. Relax the uterus: nitroglycerin 50–100 mcg IV, or terbutaline 0.25 mg SC.
  3. Replace immediately — cup the fundus and push steadily along the axis of the vagina toward the umbilicus. The longer it stays out, the harder it gets.
  4. Once replaced, restart uterotonics and keep your hand in place until the uterus contracts around it.

Eclampsia

Seizure in pregnancy or up to 6 weeks postpartum. Can present with normal or near-normal pressures.

  1. Magnesium sulfate 4–6 g IV over 15–20 min, then 1–2 g/hr. Recurrent seizure → additional 2 g bolus.
  2. Left lateral decubitus, airway protection, high-flow O₂.
  3. Severe-range BP → labetalol, hydralazine, or nifedipine per the dose table.
  4. Watch for magnesium toxicity: loss of deep tendon reflexes first, then respiratory depression. Antidote is calcium gluconate 1 g IV.
  5. Definitive treatment is delivery.

Before they leave the department

Both patients get admitted. This is what gets forgotten in the calm afterward.

Mother

  • Fundus firm and at or below the umbilicus, rechecked
  • Estimated blood loss quantified, not eyeballed
  • Perineum, vagina and cervix inspected and repaired or referred
  • Rh status checked → anti-D if negative
  • Placenta inspected and sent
  • Oxytocin infusion running, uterotonics given documented

Baby

  • ID bands on both, matched, before separation
  • Vitamin K IM
  • Erythromycin eye ointment
  • Hepatitis B vaccine
  • Glucose checked, temperature normothermic
  • Weight, Apgars, cord blood sent

Chart

  • Time of delivery, sex, presentation
  • Apgar at 1 and 5 minutes
  • Nuchal cord, meconium, complications and times
  • Every maneuver used, in order, with times — this is the record that matters if there is an injury
  • Who was present and when they arrived
  • Disposition and receiving service for both patients

Emergency DepartmentTube Thoracostomy

Confirm the sideUltrasound firstAnalgesia is not optionalNothing below the nipple line

Almost every serious complication of this procedure comes from one of three things: the wrong side, going too low, or never confirming you were in the pleural space. None of those are technique problems. They are checking problems.

Does it need a tube? The procedure When it goes wrong Doses & sizes Reading the drain Before they leave

Does this need a tube right now? — 3 checks

TensionHypotension, unilateral absent breath sounds, distended neck veins, hypoxia. Decompress now. Do not wait for a film.
UnstableTraumatic haemo- or pneumothorax with any physiological compromise, or a patient going to positive pressure ventilation.
Stable and smallAsymptomatic, small, occult on CT. Not every pneumothorax needs a tube. Observation with oxygen is a legitimate plan.
In arrest or peri-arrest trauma, go straight to finger thoracostomy — bilaterally. It is faster than a needle, it always reaches the pleura, and it tells you whether there was anything there. A needle that is too short tells you nothing.

The procedure

Open technique. Seldinger pigtail steps are called out where they differ.

force you apply correct position ✕never Diagrams are schematic. They show landmarks and direction of travel, not anatomy.

Pick the tube

before you open anything
  • Air alone: 8–14 Fr pigtail is enough. Bigger tubes hurt more and drain air no better.
  • Blood or trauma: 28–32 Fr traditionally. Randomised data support 14 Fr pigtails as non-inferior for traumatic haemothorax — reasonable in a stable patient, less so when you are chasing clot.
  • Empyema: 12–14 Fr pigtail plus intrapleural fibrinolytics beats a big tube alone.
  • Malignant effusion: pigtail, or refer for an indwelling pleural catheter rather than committing them to an inpatient stay.
Ask firstAnticoagulants? Previous thoracic surgery or pleurodesis? Known bullous disease? Any of these changes your approach or your site.

Position and mark

triangle of safety
  • Supine, head up 30–45°, arm abducted and hand behind the head. This opens the axilla and pulls the scapula back.
  • Scan before you cut. Confirm the side, look for lung sliding, find the diaphragm, and identify the liver or spleen so you know where the floor is.
  • Target the 4th or 5th intercostal space, mid-axillary line — level with the nipple in men, the inframammary fold in women.
  • Mark the skin. Say the side out loud with a second person and check it against the imaging.

Anaesthetise properly

2–5 min, worth every second
  • This is among the most painful things done to a conscious patient in the department. Under-treating it is the single most common failure of the procedure.
  • Infiltrate in layers: skin, subcutaneous tissue, the periosteum of the rib, and then the parietal pleura — the pleura is the pain, so put the last few millilitres there and wait.
  • Aspirate as you advance. When you draw back air or blood, you are through the pleura — pull back a few millimetres and deposit.
  • Add systemic analgesia. Fentanyl plus ketamine is a reasonable default in a patient who can still protect their airway.

Incision and tract

over the top of the rib
  • 2–3 cm transverse incision directly over the rib below your target space, parallel to the ribs.
  • Blunt dissect with a large curved clamp, spreading in the plane of the intercostal muscles, walking over the superior border of the rib. The neurovascular bundle runs in the groove under each rib.
  • Push the closed tip through the parietal pleura with controlled force, then spread. Expect a rush of air or blood.
  • Seldinger: introducer needle over the rib, aspirate to confirm, wire, small skin nick, dilate the tract, then the catheter over the wire. Never advance the dilator further than the tract you need.

Finger sweep

before any tube goes in
  • Gloved finger into the tract and sweep a full circle. This is the step people skip and the step that prevents the disasters.
  • You are confirming four things: you are inside the pleural space, the lung is there and moving, there are no adhesions in the way, and there is no liver or spleen under your fingertip.
  • Break down any adhesions you meet. If you feel solid organ, stop and reassess your level.

Tube and connect

all holes inside
  • Clamp the tip, guide it along your finger, then release and direct it: apical and posterior for air, posterior and basal for fluid.
  • Advance until every fenestration is inside the pleural space — the last side hole sitting in the chest wall gives you subcutaneous emphysema and a tube that will not drain.
  • Connect to the drainage system. Look for fogging, tidaling, and an initial swing or rush.
  • Secure with a simple anchoring stitch and a separate loose closing stitch. No purse-string — it causes necrosis and an ugly scar without improving the seal.
  • Occlusive dressing, tape the connection, and get a chest film.

Removal

when criteria are met
  • Criteria: no air leak, lung fully expanded on film, drainage below roughly 200 mL per day, and no ongoing indication.
  • Have the patient hold at end-expiration or Valsalva. Remove briskly in one movement and seal immediately with an occlusive dressing.
  • Repeat film afterwards. A small recurrence is common and usually does not need a new tube.

Hard stops

Short list. Each one has killed someone.

Never

  • Never go below the nipple line — that is diaphragm, liver and spleen.
  • Never use a trocar. There is no situation where it is the right tool.
  • Never clamp a bubbling tube — you have just made a tension pneumothorax.
  • Never insert without confirming the side against imaging and a second person.
  • Never skip the finger sweep on an open technique.
  • Never drain a huge chronic effusion fast — re-expansion oedema.
  • Never wait for a film in a tensioning patient.
  • Never leave a side hole outside the pleural space.

When it goes wrong

Work through these in order — most are mechanical before they are pathological.

Continuous bubbling

Bubbling through the water seal that does not stop between breaths. An air leak somewhere between alveolus and canister.

  1. Start outside and work in. Check every connection and the canister itself first — loose connections are the commonest cause.
  2. Briefly occlude the tube at the skin. If the bubbling stops, the leak is in the patient. If it continues, the leak is in your tubing.
  3. Check that no fenestration is sitting outside the chest wall — look at the film, feel the tube.
  4. Persistent large leak with poor expansion → suspect bronchopleural fistula or tracheobronchial injury. Call thoracic surgery.

Nothing draining, no tidaling

A still water seal column. Either the tube is blocked, or the pleural space is empty.

  1. Look at the tube along its whole length for kinks, and under the patient for a compressed segment.
  2. Milk the tube gently for clot. Do not strip aggressively — the negative pressure generated is enormous.
  3. Get a film. Malposition in the fissure, in the soft tissues, or partly withdrawn all look like this.
  4. If the film shows a fully expanded lung and the patient is well, the tube has simply finished its job.

Massive haemothorax

Big immediate output or ongoing brisk bleeding. This is a surgical decision, and it is time-critical.

  1. >1500 mL immediately on insertion, or
  2. >200 mL/hr for 2–4 hours, or
  3. ongoing transfusion requirement with haemodynamic instability.
  4. Any of these → thoracotomy. Call surgery while the tube is still going in, not after.
  5. Activate massive transfusion, warm everything, and do not autotransfuse into a coagulopathy without a plan.

Re-expansion pulmonary oedema

Cough, breathlessness and hypoxia during or shortly after drainage. Usually the side you just drained.

  1. Highest risk with large volumes, lungs collapsed for more than 72 hours, and young patients.
  2. Clamp after 1–1.5 L and wait an hour before draining more. Stop early for chest tightness or cough.
  3. Treatment is supportive: oxygen, non-invasive or invasive ventilation as needed. Diuretics do not help and can harm.
  4. Most resolve within 24–48 hours, but it can be fatal.

Subcutaneous emphysema

Crackling tissue spreading across the chest wall, neck and face.

  1. Usually a side hole outside the pleura, an occluded tube with an ongoing leak, or a tube too small for the leak.
  2. Check patency and position first; reposition or upsize rather than adding suction blindly.
  3. Watch the airway if it reaches the neck and face. Rapidly progressive emphysema needs an urgent airway assessment.
  4. Rarely needs specific treatment beyond fixing the drainage.

Tube falls out

Partial or complete dislodgement, often when the patient is moved.

  1. Do not push it back in. The external portion is contaminated.
  2. Cover the site with an occlusive dressing taped on three sides so air can escape but not enter.
  3. Reassess clinically and with a film. If there is still an indication, place a new tube through a fresh site.
  4. Watch closely for tension in the interval, particularly if the patient is on positive pressure.

Reading the drain

Three chambers, three questions: how much, is it patent, is there a leak.

Normal expectationsAn initial rush, then swinging with respiration, then a slow tail-off. Output that stops abruptly in the first hours is a blocked tube until proven otherwise.

Before they leave the department

Most of these take seconds and are the difference between a good tube and a callback.

Confirm

  • Chest film reviewed by you, not just ordered
  • Tube position and all fenestrations intrapleural
  • Lung re-expanded, or a reason why not
  • Tidaling present, leak status documented
  • Initial output volume and character recorded
  • Suction level set and stated in the notes

Secure

  • Anchoring suture placed, no purse-string
  • Occlusive dressing and taped connections
  • Canister below the level of the chest, upright
  • Clamps at the bedside for disconnection only
  • Patient told not to lie on the tubing
  • Ongoing analgesia prescribed, not just given once

Chart

  • Side, site, size, technique, and who did it
  • Consent and time out, including side confirmation
  • Anaesthetic type and total dose
  • Immediate output and any complication
  • Antibiotic given or explicitly not indicated
  • Receiving team notified and criteria for escalation

Emergency Department · CICOCricothyrotomy

Declare it out loudScalpel · finger · bougie6.0 cuffed tubeUnder 30 seconds

People do not die of a difficult cricothyrotomy. They die of a late one. The technical steps take under a minute to learn and under thirty seconds to perform — the hard part is saying the words "this is a can't intubate, can't oxygenate situation" while there is still time for them to matter.

Is this CICO? — 3 failures

Intubation failedBest attempt by the best operator available, with optimal position and a bougie. Not four attempts by four people.
Supraglottic failedA correctly sized device, correctly seated, does not deliver ventilation.
Face mask failedTwo-person technique, adjuncts in, jaw thrust, and you still cannot oxygenate.
All three failed means you are already behind. Do not wait for the saturation to bottom out or for someone senior to arrive. Declare it, hand the mask to someone else so oxygenation continues, and go to the neck.

The procedure

Scalpel, finger, bougie. Four pieces of kit and one continuous movement.

force you apply correct position ✕never Diagrams are schematic. They show landmarks and direction of travel, not anatomy.

Declare and position

seconds, not minutes
  • Say it in plain words: "This is a CICO situation. I am performing a cricothyrotomy." This is not ceremony — it stops the room from continuing to attempt intubation around you.
  • Assign roles out loud: one person keeps oxygenating, one hands you kit, one watches the clock and the monitor.
  • Extend the neck and put a roll under the shoulders. If there is a cervical spine concern, the airway still wins.
  • Stand on the patient's side at the level of the shoulder, dominant hand free.
  • Keep oxygen flowing throughout: nasal cannula at 15 L, mask or supraglottic left in place.
The actual failure modeContinuing to attempt intubation after CICO has been reached. If you have declared it, the laryngoscope goes down and stays down.

Find the membrane

laryngeal handshake
  • Non-dominant hand: thumb and middle finger on either side of the upper larynx, index finger free. Rock the larynx gently side to side to feel its shape.
  • Walk the index finger down the midline over the thyroid lamina until it drops into the soft gap below the thyroid cartilage and above the cricoid ring. That gap is the cricothyroid membrane — roughly a centimetre tall and two to three wide.
  • Once you have it, that hand does not move again until the tube is in. Everything else happens around it.
  • If you genuinely cannot feel it, do not keep palpating. Go straight to the vertical incision described below.

Scalpel, then bougie

one continuous movement
  • Stab a #10 blade transversely through skin and membrane in one motion. Commit — a timid incision that stops in the subcutaneous tissue costs you the landmark and fills the field with blood.
  • Rotate the blade 90° so the sharp edge points towards the feet. The blade now holds the hole open for you.
  • Slide the bougie along the caudal side of the blade, angled towards the feet, into the trachea. Tracheal clicks or a distal hold-up tell you that you are in the airway and not in a pretracheal plane.
  • Only now remove the scalpel. Keep the bougie and your stabilising hand exactly where they are.

Tube — and stop early

shallow, not deep
  • Railroad a 6.0 cuffed tube over the bougie, rotating gently as it passes the skin and membrane.
  • Advance only until the cuff is through, then a centimetre or two more. The distance from membrane to carina is short. A tube inserted to its normal depth mark sits in the right main bronchus.
  • Inflate the cuff, remove the bougie, ventilate.
  • Confirm with waveform capnography. Chest rise, misting and a rising saturation are supportive but not sufficient — a pretracheal tube can produce all three briefly.
  • Secure with ties and a dedicated holder. Do not let anyone move the patient until it is fixed.

If you cannot feel the landmarks

go long and go blunt
  • Obesity, haematoma, oedema, surgical change and previous radiotherapy all take the anatomy away from your fingertips. Do not keep palpating — that is where the minutes go.
  • Vertical midline incision, 8–10 cm, from the thyroid cartilage downwards. Long is safer than short: you stay in the midline where there are no major vessels, and you can extend in either direction.
  • Blunt finger dissection down to the larynx. Spread tissues apart, then palpate with the fingertip until you find the cartilage and the soft gap.
  • Once you have identified the membrane by feel, the technique is exactly the same: stab, rotate, bougie, tube.
  • Ultrasound is excellent for marking the membrane in a difficult neck — but only if it is already on and someone else is driving it.

Hard stops

Short list, and every item is a real reported death or disaster.

Never

  • Never keep attempting laryngoscopy once CICO has been declared.
  • Never let go of the larynx until the tube is in and the cuff is up.
  • Never insert the tube to normal depth — that is the right main bronchus.
  • Never do an open cricothyrotomy in a small child — needle technique instead.
  • Never accept chest rise alone as confirmation. Waveform capnography or keep looking.
  • Never ventilate through a needle without a route for gas to escape.
  • Never abandon a correctly sited tube because the field is bleeding — ventilate first, then deal with the blood.
  • Never remove the scalpel before the bougie is in the trachea.

When it goes wrong

Almost all of these are recoverable if you notice them in the first minute.

False passage

The tube is in the neck but not in the airway. Pretracheal, paratracheal, or through into the mediastinum. This is what kills.

  1. No waveform capnography means you are not in the airway until proven otherwise, whatever else you can see.
  2. Suspect it with rising resistance, absent chest rise, and rapidly expanding surgical emphysema on ventilation.
  3. Do not keep bagging into a false passage — it dissects the planes and destroys your remaining landmarks.
  4. Take the tube out, re-identify the membrane with your finger, and start again through the same hole with the bougie.
  5. Prevent it: feel for tracheal clicks or hold-up on the bougie before railroading anything.

Wrong level

Too high is the thyrohyoid membrane; too low is the cricoid ring or the tracheal rings and the isthmus.

  1. Above the thyroid cartilage → you are in the pre-epiglottic space and will not reach the airway.
  2. Through the cricoid ring itself → cartilage fracture and long-term subglottic stenosis.
  3. Below the cricoid → the thyroid isthmus bleeds heavily and the trachea is deeper and more mobile.
  4. Recover by returning to the handshake: find the hard shield above and the hard ring below, and go into the soft gap between them.

Bleeding

Anterior jugular veins and the thyroid isthmus. The field goes red immediately and you cannot see anything.

  1. Expect it and ignore it. This is a tactile procedure, not a visual one — your finger and the bougie do not need a clear field.
  2. Do not stop to suction or pack before the tube is in. Oxygenation first, haemostasis second.
  3. Once ventilating, pack around the tube and apply direct pressure. Most venous bleeding settles.
  4. Arterial or isthmus bleeding that persists needs surgical exploration — call ENT or surgery early.

Bougie will not pass

It meets resistance a centimetre or two in, or slides in freely with no clicks and no hold-up.

  1. Free passage with no hold-up suggests a pretracheal plane, not the airway. Withdraw and reassess.
  2. Hard resistance suggests the blade has rotated, the hole has closed, or you are against the posterior wall — flatten your angle and aim more caudally.
  3. Put the scalpel back in to hold the tract open if you removed it early.
  4. If the anatomy has been lost entirely, convert to a vertical incision and find the larynx with your finger.

Posterior wall injury

Aiming straight back rather than down. The oesophagus sits immediately behind.

  1. Prevent it: once through the membrane, angle everything towards the feet, not towards the spine.
  2. Limit blade insertion depth — a finger's breadth from the tip is plenty.
  3. Suspect it with gastric distension on ventilation, or air in the mediastinum on later imaging.
  4. Ventilate through a confirmed airway first, then involve surgery. Injuries are usually repaired at a later operation.

Afterwards

A cricothyrotomy is a temporary airway placed under the worst conditions available.

  1. Arrange conversion to a formal tracheostomy or oral intubation once the patient is stable — usually within 24 hours, though evidence for a strict deadline is weak.
  2. Involve ENT or surgery early, and image the neck when the patient can tolerate it.
  3. Debrief the team. This is a rare, high-stress event and the learning evaporates within hours.
  4. Complete an incident or airway registry form. These cases are how departments improve their airway systems.

Afterwards

The tube is in. These are the things that get missed in the relief.

Confirm

  • Sustained waveform capnography, not a single trace
  • Bilateral chest rise and equal air entry
  • Saturation and heart rate recovering
  • No expanding surgical emphysema on ventilation
  • Cuff pressure checked once stable
  • Chest film for depth and for a pneumothorax

Secure and plan

  • Tube tied and held — this one falls out easily
  • Depth at the skin recorded and marked
  • Sedation and analgesia running
  • ENT or surgery contacted for definitive airway
  • Bleeding site inspected and packed if needed
  • Bed booked — this patient is going to critical care

Chart and debrief

  • Time CICO was declared and time of first breath
  • Every airway attempt before it, and by whom
  • Technique used, tube size, complications
  • Confirmation method documented explicitly
  • Team debrief before anyone leaves the shift
  • Airway registry or incident form completed
