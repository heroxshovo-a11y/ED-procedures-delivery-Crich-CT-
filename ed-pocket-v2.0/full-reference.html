Start here: two patients, two leads

ED delivery and newborn resuscitation | Physician cognitive aid

Birth imminent: prepare to deliver where you are. Baby apneic/gasping or HR <100: open Newborn resuscitation. Head out, shoulders stuck: open Shoulder dystocia. Maternal bleeding/shock: open Postpartum hemorrhage.

Activate the pathway immediately

  1. Call the receiving OB service and neonatal team/transport now. Request remote support and activate transfer if none is on site. Call anesthesia, a second ED clinician, RT, extra nursing and surgery as available. State whether delivery is occurring now.
  2. Assign a mother lead and a baby lead by name. The baby needs a dedicated clinician able to provide neonatal ventilation. Assign a timer/recorder and medication/access person. Two leads are role assignments, not adequate staffing for simultaneous advanced resuscitations. Recruit extra staff and state gaps aloud.
  3. Decide deliver here versus transfer before birth. Crowning, a presenting part at the outlet, or involuntary pushing with advanced descent makes transport unsafe. If birth is not imminent and mother/fetus can tolerate transport, arrange prompt transfer with a delivery-capable escort and neonatal kit. A contraction interval alone does not establish imminence.
  4. Ask while preparing: estimated gestation, parity, prior rapid birth or cesarean, singleton/twins, presentation, placenta problems, rupture time/fluid, bleeding, prenatal complications, allergies, hypertension, asthma, and relevant maternal drugs. Obtain GBS, HBsAg, HIV and blood-group results if available. If time permits, assess fetal HR and compare with maternal pulse; do not delay imminent birth for monitoring.

Prepare in parallel; do not delay a crowning birth

Precipitous vaginal delivery

Cephalic birth at the outlet | Mother lead

Do not restrain an emerging baby or transfer a crowning patient. Call out the time of head delivery and complete birth. Resistance to gentle delivery of the shoulders means shoulder dystocia.
  1. Position and support. Explain; position for access with buttocks supported near the bed edge, semi-recumbent or another tolerated birth position. Avoid flat supine positioning if hypotensive. Prepare to receive a slippery infant.
  2. Deliver the head with control. Encourage pushing with contractions and panting as the head crowns. Support the perineum and use gentle support of the emerging head; allow extension. Do not forcibly hold the head back, apply fundal pressure, or perform routine episiotomy.
  3. Check for a nuchal cord. If loose, gently slip it over the head or shoulders. If tight, keep the head close to the maternal perineum/thigh while the shoulders and body deliver in a somersault, then unwind the cord. Do not pull on it. Double-clamp and cut before body delivery only if the intact cord truly prevents birth and an intact-cord maneuver fails; complete delivery promptly and anticipate neonatal hypovolemia/resuscitation.
  4. Allow restitution and spontaneous shoulders. Let the head rotate naturally; allow birth with the next contraction if there is no impaction. If assistance is needed, support the head in line with the fetal spine and use only gentle axial traction. Never lever or twist the neck. Failure to release, or tight head retraction, means declare shoulder dystocia and change maneuvers immediately; do not wait for another contraction once impaction is recognized.
  5. Receive the body. Support the head, neck and trunk with both hands and a dry towel. Do not suspend the baby by the ankles. Record birth time. The baby lead immediately assesses breathing/cry, tone and gestation.
  6. Manage the cord and warmth. A stable newborn not needing immediate resuscitation: defer clamping for at least 60 seconds and keep warm, usually skin-to-skin on the parent. There is no requirement to hold the baby below the perineum. If essential resuscitation cannot be delivered with the cord intact, clamp/cut promptly and move to the warmer; maternal collapse, disrupted placental circulation or major cord bleeding also warrants prompt action.
  7. Clamp safely. Exclude abdominal-wall tissue from clamps. Leave several centimeters of stump if UVC access may be needed; double-clamp and cut between. Check hemostasis. The UVC operator trims separately. Do not routinely milk the cord; never milk an intact cord below 28 weeks.
  8. Mother lead, in parallel: after birth and excluding another baby, give prophylactic oxytocin promptly, preferably within 1 min (10 units IM once, or the standardized IV regimen); do not wait for cord cutting. Watch for placental separation: cord lengthening, a small gush and a firm rising uterus. A trained operator may use gentle controlled cord traction with suprapubic counter-support during contraction. Stop for resistance/inversion; otherwise await spontaneous placenta. Never pull forcefully.
  9. Reassess the mother. Quantify cumulative blood loss, assess uterine tone, inspect the placenta/membranes and examine for trauma. Massage a boggy uterus; a well-contracted uterus after prophylactic oxytocin does not need continuous routine massage. Bleeding/shock triggers the PPH pathway immediately. A placenta still undelivered at 30 minutes needs urgent OB planning; bleeding makes intervention urgent sooner.
No routine suction before or after complete birth, including meconium-stained fluid. Clear an airway only when obstruction is suspected. Dry/stimulate quickly; do not let suction, Apgar scoring or cord sampling delay PPV.

Newborn resuscitation

Birth transition | AHA/AAP 2025 sequence

Ventilation is the priority. Start PPV by 60 seconds of life for apnea, gasping, or HR <100/min after initial steps. A rising heart rate is the best sign that ventilation is working.
  1. At birth: term, good tone, breathing/crying? All yes: warm skin-to-skin care, defer clamping at least 60 seconds and observe. Any concern: assess/support at a warm surface. Preterm infants need thermal/respiratory assessment even if breathing.
  2. Initial steps, completed rapidly. Warm, position head neutral/slightly extended, dry/stimulate. Below 32 weeks: wrap the wet body without drying; face exposed, hat and radiant heat. Suction only for obstruction. Target temperature 36.5-37.5°C.
  3. Apnea/gasping or HR <100: begin PPV 30-60 breaths/min. Apply right-hand/wrist oximeter and consider ECG; do not wait for monitors. Auscultate initial HR. Correct leak/obstruction immediately for absent chest movement or HR response.
  4. After 30 seconds of ventilation that inflates the lungs: reassess HR using the decision table. Time counts from effective lung inflation, not from ineffective mask attempts.
  5. HR <60: optimize the airway (ETT preferred; neonatal SGA if appropriate and intubation fails), start coordinated 3 compressions : 1 breath, use 100% oxygen, apply ECG and obtain UVC/IO access. Deliver 90 compressions plus 30 breaths each minute.
  6. After 60 seconds of coordinated compressions and effective ventilation: if HR remains <60, give intravascular epinephrine. Continue CPR/ventilation, reassess about every 60 seconds and repeat epinephrine every 3-5 minutes if HR remains <60. Recheck airway/ventilation and consider hypovolemia or pneumothorax.
  7. Recovery: when HR is at least 60 with circulation, stop compressions and continue PPV as needed. At HR at least 100 with adequate spontaneous breathing, wean PPV and titrate oxygen down to target. Arrange monitored neonatal care/transport.
HR / breathing after effective PPVAction
At least 100/minAdequate breathing: wean PPV. Apnea: continue PPV. Distress/hypoxemia: assess oxygen/CPAP need.

Compression technique: two thumbs on the lower third of the sternum, above the xiphoid; hands encircle the chest. Depth one-third of the chest’s AP diameter; full recoil. Count “one-and-two-and-three-and-breathe.” Keep 3:1 even with an ETT. Verify an audible HR/pulse if ECG suggests recovery; ECG alone does not prove perfusion.

Apgar documents; it does not trigger treatment. Do not wait for scores, glucose or oximetry to ventilate. This is the birth-transition pathway; a later infant arrest may require PALS.

Newborn ventilation

Make the chest move and the heart rate rise

Start PPV

  • Mask: seal around nose/mouth, lower rim on the chin and upper rim below the eyes; do not overlap below the chin. Support the bony jaw without soft-tissue pressure; keep the neck neutral/slightly extended. Use a small shoulder roll only if needed; avoid hyperextension.
  • Device: tested T-piece if available; otherwise a neonatal self-inflating bag (works without a compressed-gas source). With pressure control, start PIP 20-30 cm H2O; titrate to HR response and gentle chest movement. PEEP 5 cm H2O is a common NRP starting setting; adjust to the infant/device and local neonatal protocol. Do not delay PPV because a manometer, blender or PEEP valve is unavailable.
  • Rate: 30-60/min; inflation time about 0.5-1 second. Avoid excessive volume/pressure and prolonged sustained inflations. Confirm chest movement promptly; do not spend 30 seconds providing ineffective breaths.

Newborn advanced airway

SGA rescue, intubation and tube depth

  1. Neonatal supraglottic airway: reasonable at 34 weeks or more when mask ventilation fails. Choose size by manufacturer (often size 1 for a term infant); weight limits vary. Insert along the palate per device technique, ventilate and confirm chest movement/HR response. AHA/AAP supports SGA rescue when intubation is unsuccessful; evidence in smaller preterm infants is limited.
  2. ETT: prepare suction, straight blade 00/0/1 as appropriate, neonatal CO2 detector and uncuffed ETT. Typical internal diameter: <1 kg = 2.5 mm; 1-2 kg = 3.0 mm; >2 kg = 3.5 mm, with 4.0 mm sometimes used in a large term infant. Lift the epiglottis under direct or video vision and pass the tube through the cords.
  3. Keep attempts brief (about 30 seconds maximum); stop sooner for deterioration and resume effective mask/SGA ventilation. Confirm with exhaled CO2 plus chest movement, rising HR and bilateral air entry. Very low pulmonary blood flow can cause a false-negative CO2 detector. Use the gestation/weight depth table as an initial estimate at the upper lip; confirm depth clinically and by radiograph once stable. Do not use adult or age-based tube formulas.
Spontaneous breathing with distress: consider CPAP, particularly for preterm infants; it does not treat apnea. Term/late-preterm CPAP has less certain benefit and can increase pneumothorax risk. A self-inflating bag does not reliably provide CPAP or free-flow oxygen. Intubate/suction meconium only if obstruction prevents effective ventilation.

Emergency umbilical venous access

Short, low UVC for resuscitation | Keep ventilation going

UVC is the preferred emergency neonatal vascular route. If not promptly feasible, use a neonatal-appropriate IO device/site according to training and manufacturer limits. Do not allow repeated access attempts to interrupt ventilation/CPR.

Prepare

Sterile gloves/drape, neonatal-safe antiseptic, cord tie, scalpel, forceps, single-lumen 3.5 or 5 Fr umbilical catheter, extension/three-way stopcock, saline and syringe. Use a smaller catheter for a small preterm infant. Prime catheter and tubing completely; keep all unused ports closed to prevent air embolism.

  1. Expose and prepare the stump. Keep the infant warm and chest accessible. Apply neonatal-appropriate antiseptic without pooling on fragile skin. Place a loose cord tie at the base for bleeding control; avoid skin.
  2. Trim the cord. Cut cleanly across about 1-2 cm above the abdominal skin, preserving the tie. Tighten only enough to control bleeding while allowing catheter passage.
  3. Identify the vein. Find the single larger, thin-walled, usually gaping vessel; the two arteries are smaller, thicker-walled and often constricted. Do not select a vessel by clock position alone.
  4. Insert only far enough for free blood return. Gently pass the primed catheter into the vein, aspirating as you advance. Use a short, low emergency position; stop as soon as blood aspirates freely. A fixed insertion distance is not a substitute for this functional endpoint. Do not force resistance or advance toward a central position during resuscitation.
  5. Confirm function before using. Require free blood return, a gentle saline flush without resistance and no abdominal swelling/blanching or leakage. If there is no return, withdraw slightly/reassess; do not inject blindly. A functional short emergency UVC can be used for resuscitation before radiographic confirmation.
  6. Give the drug and appropriate flush. Intravascular epinephrine uses 0.1 mg/mL solution and is followed by 3 mL normal saline. Close the system and continue compressions/ventilation. Have a second clinician read back concentration, dose, volume and route.
  7. Secure and hand over. Record catheter size and depth at skin/stump, patency and drugs/fluids given. Continuously observe for bleeding and extravasation. This is temporary low access; arrange definitive access/position assessment with the receiving neonatal team before ongoing central infusions.
Do not confuse a low emergency UVC with a central UVC. Central placement needs a separate depth calculation and imaging confirmation; malposition in the liver/portal circulation can cause major injury. A low UVC should not be used for parenteral nutrition or other prolonged hyperosmolar infusions.

If access is delayed

ETT epinephrine may be used as a bridge through a confirmed endotracheal tube while UVC/IO is obtained; absorption is less reliable and the dose is higher. Do not give the ETT dose via an SGA or intravascularly. If the ETT dose is ineffective, give the intravascular dose when access is ready, without waiting out a 3-5 minute interval.

Poor response or post-resuscitation stabilization

After D10W rescue: prevent recurrence

A CPS starting infusion example is D10W at 80 mL/kg/day = 3.3 mL/kg/h (about 5.5 mg/kg/min glucose), using a pump. For a 3-kg infant this is about 10 mL/h. For an unwell or symptomatic infant in the first 72 h, CPS uses a treatment goal of at least 2.6 mmol/L (47 mg/dL); very low values below 1.8 mmol/L (32 mg/dL) need urgent IV treatment. This is not a complete well-baby screening algorithm; local age-specific thresholds may differ.

Persistent HR <60: check mask/ETT position, lung inflation, gas supply and equipment; verify drug delivery. Consider hemorrhage (abruption, cord bleeding, fetomaternal hemorrhage) and tension pneumothorax. A pale infant with poor perfusion may need blood. Sudden asymmetric ventilation/deterioration needs urgent assessment and neonatal decompression if tension is suspected.

Not routine neonatal resuscitation drugs: sodium bicarbonate, naloxone and atropine. Respiratory depression after maternal opioids is managed with ventilation. Post-resuscitation glucose treatment supports stabilization; it must not displace the ventilation/CPR sequence.

Shoulder dystocia

Head delivered; shoulders fail to release with gentle axial traction

Declare shoulder dystocia. Start the clock. Stop repeated traction. No fundal pressure, forceful downward pull or lateral bending of the fetal neck. Do not wait for a 60-second interval to diagnose it.
  1. Call and position. Call additional OB/surgical, neonatal and nursing help; place a remote obstetric expert on speaker if needed. Ask the patient to stop pushing briefly while repositioning. Flatten the bed, remove pillows, and bring buttocks to the edge so there is room for internal maneuvers. Stop an oxytocin infusion if running for labor.
  2. McRoberts. Two assistants flex both hips tightly, bringing thighs onto the abdomen. Lift the buttocks slightly through effective hip flexion. The delivering clinician reassesses using only routine gentle axial traction.
  3. Add suprapubic pressure. Identify the side of the fetal back. An assistant presses just above the pubic symphysis from that side, downward and laterally against the back of the anterior shoulder, toward the fetal chest. This adducts/rotates the shoulder. This is pressure over the pubic bone, not over the uterine fundus.
  4. If unreleased, move promptly to an internal maneuver. Enter the roomy posterior sacral hollow with the whole gloved hand. Choose posterior arm delivery or internal rotation based on access, training and circumstances. Do not keep repeating ineffective McRoberts/suprapubic pressure while waiting for help.
  5. Posterior arm. Find the posterior shoulder and trace the arm to the elbow/wrist. Flex the elbow if needed, then gently sweep the forearm across the fetal chest/face and out of the vagina. Avoid pulling on the humeral shaft; fracture can occur. Reassess shoulder release with gentle axial traction.
  6. Internal rotation. Apply pressure to a shoulder, not the head, to turn the shoulders into an oblique pelvic diameter. Rubin: pressure on the posterior surface of a shoulder toward the fetal chest. Woods: pressure on the front of the posterior shoulder to rotate it; reverse direction if the opposite rotation is more accessible. Coordinate external suprapubic pressure. Reassess after a purposeful attempt.
  7. All fours (Gaskin). If she can be turned safely, move to hands-and-knees and reassess delivery. This may be used earlier when mobility/staffing favors it. Repeat useful internal maneuvers with a new position/operator if appropriate; communicate each change and elapsed time.
  8. Still trapped: obtain immediate senior rescue guidance while continuing active maneuvers. Digital posterior axillary traction may be considered by a trained operator. Cephalic replacement (Zavanelli) requires immediate operative rescue; symphysiotomy and deliberate fracture are not routine standalone ED fallback steps.
Episiotomy does not enlarge the bony pelvis. Consider it only if soft tissue prevents internal hand access. There is no safe period of inactivity and no single mandatory sequence after initial maneuvers.

After delivery: newborn resuscitation readiness; examine for arm weakness/fracture and assess neonatal acidemia/encephalopathy as indicated. Reassess mother for PPH and anal sphincter injury. Record head/body times, maneuvers and responses, team members and neonatal findings.

Umbilical cord prolapse

Relieve compression continuously while arranging the fastest safe birth

Suspect after membrane rupture with sudden fetal bradycardia, or visible/palpable cord. Call for immediate operative delivery/transfer and neonatal support while you decompress the cord.
  1. Lift the presenting part, not the cord. Insert a gloved hand vaginally and elevate the fetal head/breech off the cord. Use enough lift to relieve pressure, without excessive displacement. Maintain decompression continuously during preparation and transport; arrange a deliberate hand-to-hand change if personnel must change.
  2. Position to reduce compression. Use knee-chest while stationary or left lateral with head-down tilt when feasible. A safely secured lateral position is more practical during ambulance transport. Do not allow positioning to delay definitive care.
  3. Minimize cord handling. Do not push it back above the presenting part, stretch it or repeatedly palpate it. A loose warm saline-moistened covering may be used for exposed cord; benefit is unproven and it must not delay decompression or birth.
  4. Stop uterine stimulation. Stop oxytocin if infusing. Monitor fetal heart rate if available without interrupting decompression. Maternal oxygen is for maternal hypoxemia, not routine treatment of an abnormal fetal tracing in a normoxic patient.
  5. If a long delay makes manual elevation impractical: bladder filling is an alternative. Instill 500-750 mL sterile normal saline through a Foley, clamp, and confirm presenting-part elevation/cord decompression before withdrawing the hand. Do not add it routinely to effective manual elevation. Drain the bladder before any vaginal or cesarean delivery attempt.
  6. Consider tocolysis only as an adjunct for persistent compression/bradycardia when birth is delayed: terbutaline 0.25 mg SC once, with obstetric guidance and maternal monitoring. Do not delay delivery to give it.
  7. Choose the quickest achievable safe birth. Cesarean is usual if vaginal birth is not immediately achievable. If fully dilated with the head at the outlet and rapid vaginal birth is feasible, that may be faster. Instrumental birth requires a qualified operator and its usual prerequisites; cord prolapse is not an automatic instruction to defer an already imminent vaginal birth.

Handover before movement

  • State who is maintaining decompression and who takes over at arrival.
  • Give rupture/prolapse time, fetal heart-rate trend, cervical/presentation findings, maternal status and interventions.
  • Report any bladder instillation volume and the explicit need to drain before birth.
  • Bring a neonatal ventilation kit and staff able to use it. Do not wait for a normal tracing before proceeding with transfer/operative planning.

Unexpected breech delivery

If birth is not imminent, obtain urgent obstetric transfer/operative planning

Do not pull a breech infant out. Call buttocks and umbilicus times. Assist promptly for poor tone or no progress with a contraction/pushing. Over 3 min from umbilicus to head, or 5 min from buttocks to head, signals delay: never wait for these limits if descent has stopped.
  1. Call and assess. Activate OB/surgical and neonatal assistance. Note gestation, whether the cervix is fully dilated, and whether delivery can still occur in a higher-capability setting. Footling breech has particular cord-prolapse risk. Do not attempt an elective vaginal breech delivery from this card.
  2. Allow spontaneous descent. Encourage effective pushing with contractions. Initially keep hands off except for support; avoid traction that extends the head/arms. Keep the fetal back anterior/upward as the body emerges. If handling is required, hold the bony pelvis through a towel, not the abdomen/flanks.
  3. Legs if retained and obstructing birth: a trained operator can flex a knee with gentle pressure in the popliteal fossa and sweep the leg down/out (Pinard maneuver), one leg at a time. Do not pull on an extended leg.
  4. Arms. Allow spontaneous release to the scapulae. If an arm remains up, reach toward the elbow, flex it and guide the hand/forearm across the chest/face and out. If arms are extended/nuchal, use Lovset: hold the bony pelvis, keep the back anterior, and rotate the trunk to bring the posterior shoulder anterior beneath the symphysis, then release that arm. Reverse the rotation to release the other. Do not twist the neck or pull on the humeral shaft.
  5. Aftercoming head: maintain flexion. Support the baby prone astride your forearm. Place fingers on the cheekbones/maxilla, not inside the mouth and not on the mandible. The other hand supports the shoulders and applies controlled pressure to the occiput to keep the chin on the chest.
  6. Deliver the flexed head. An assistant applies suprapubic pressure to assist flexion/descent. With gentle, controlled guidance, bring the head down until the nape/hairline is under the pubic arch; then lift the supported body in an arc toward the maternal abdomen to deliver face and head. Avoid hyperextension or lifting the unsupported body before the head has descended.
  7. Head trapped: call this explicitly. Maintain flexion/support, assess for an incompletely dilated cervical rim and whether it can be gently displaced over the head; use expert-guided suprapubic pressure and the head maneuver. Uterine relaxation or cervical incisions require expert rescue planning and maternal resuscitation capability. Do not keep escalating traction or leave the infant hanging while waiting.
  8. After birth: proceed immediately to neonatal assessment/PPV as indicated; inspect for trauma and manage the maternal third stage/bleeding.
Simulation priority: rehearse hand placement for Lovset and the modified Mauriceau-Smellie-Veit maneuver with an obstetric instructor. Descriptions cannot establish competence for an entrapped head. Persistent transverse lie or a prolapsed arm without a delivering breech requires emergency obstetric operative management, not traction on the limb.

Postpartum hemorrhage

Treat the bleeding and its cause while resuscitating

Do not wait for 1,000 mL. Activate treatment for brisk ongoing bleeding or clinical compromise. WHO 2025 triggers: measured loss at least 500 mL, or at least 300 mL plus an abnormal hemodynamic sign (pulse >100, SBP <100, DBP <60, or HR/SBP >1).
  1. Call a hemorrhage emergency and start transfer/OR planning. Call OB/surgery/anesthesia, blood bank and additional staff. Assign someone to the baby. Measure cumulative loss using a calibrated drape/suction and weighed materials (about 1 g gained = 1 mL blood). Reassess vital signs and mentation frequently.
  2. Resuscitate in parallel. Two large-bore IVs; send CBC, type/crossmatch, PT/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen and blood gas/lactate. Do not wait for hemoglobin to fall. Activate MTP for ongoing major loss/shock. Give warmed blood products per local balanced protocol, monitor ionized calcium and correct hypocalcemia; keep the patient warm. Use limited warmed crystalloid while blood arrives.
  3. Assess tone; massage and compress if atonic. A boggy uterus suggests atony. Empty the bladder with a Foley. Start fundal massage, then bimanual compression if bleeding persists: one gloved hand/fist in the anterior vaginal fornix elevates the uterus while the abdominal hand compresses the uterine body/fundus against it. Use sustained compression as a bridge while others give drugs.
  4. Give oxytocin and TXA promptly. Use the maternal dose card. TXA 1 g IV over 10 min, as soon as possible and within 3 hours of birth; it is part of the first response for PPH, including traumatic bleeding. Add an appropriate second-line uterotonic for persistent atony after considering hypertension/asthma. Do not wait for a drug cycle to finish before finding the source.
  5. Find the cause: Tone, Trauma, Tissue, Thrombin. Check uterine tone and fundal position, placenta completeness, cervix/vagina/perineum and concealed hematoma. A firm uterus with continued bleeding suggests trauma or coagulopathy. A missing fundus with vaginal mass suggests inversion: stop uterotonics and replace first. Suspected rupture, accreta or uncontrolled laceration needs operative control.
  6. Escalate refractory atony. Continue bimanual compression. WHO recommends uterine balloon tamponade with trained monitoring, blood and immediate surgical access, after assessing tissue/trauma and excluding rupture. If these conditions are absent, continue compression/urgent transfer and discuss any balloon rescue with the receiving OB team. Follow device instructions; a single small Foley balloon is not equivalent to a uterine tamponade balloon.
  7. Do not let temporary improvement delay definitive control. Persistent shock, transfusion need, ongoing major bleeding, retained adherent placenta, rupture or failed tamponade requires urgent surgery/embolization capability. Continue resuscitation and a named clinician’s handoff throughout transfer.
If no balloon or definitive help is immediately available: sustained bimanual compression is a practical bridge. For uncontrollable atonic bleeding, a trained clinician may apply external aortic compression: firm pressure just above the umbilicus and slightly left of midline against the spine, confirming reduced femoral pulse and bleeding. This is temporary and must not delay blood/operative rescue.

Fibrinogen: an early low value is concerning; in major PPH, replace per protocol when <200 mg/dL (2 g/L), generally targeting at least 200 mg/dL. Monitor platelets/coagulation and calcium during MTP. Recheck uterine tone and loss even after bleeding appears controlled.

Inversion, retained placenta and tears

Persistent bleeding needs a cause-specific response

Uterine inversion: replace promptly while resuscitating

Clues: absent abdominal fundus, red/blue vaginal mass, bleeding and shock. Stop cord traction and uterotonics. Leave an attached placenta attached initially. Do not delay a first replacement attempt for a relaxant.
  1. Call OB/surgery/anesthesia and activate hemorrhage resuscitation. Give blood/support perfusion as needed. A second team continues newborn care.
  2. Immediate manual replacement (Johnson). With a gloved hand, cup the inverted fundus and apply sustained pressure up the vaginal axis toward the umbilicus, guiding the uterus through the cervical ring. Avoid excessive focal force or removing an attached placenta before reduction.
  3. If a constriction ring prevents reduction: request urgent anesthesia/OB help for relaxation and repeat reduction. Use anesthesia/OB-guided uterine relaxation with a defined drug protocol, immediate resuscitation and BP monitoring; relaxants can worsen hypotension/tachycardia. Drug preparation must not postpone manual reduction.
  4. After replacement: maintain the uterus in position until tone returns; restart oxytocin/uterotonics and continue hemorrhage care. Arrange specialist placental removal if still attached and antibiotic prophylaxis per local protocol. Failed manual reduction requires urgent specialist hydrostatic/operative rescue.

Retained or incomplete placenta

  • At 30 minutes without placental delivery: urgent OB plan for removal, even if bleeding is modest. With hemorrhage, intervene/escalate sooner. Do not wait for an arbitrary time threshold in shock.
  • Stable with limited bleeding: empty the bladder, monitor closely and arrange definitive care. Do not force cord traction or perform routine blind exploration. Do not delay transfer solely to wait for placental delivery.
  • Life-threatening bleeding from retained placenta: blood, compression as appropriate and urgent removal/source control. If an appropriately trained ED clinician must perform emergency manual removal because operative help is unavailable, use analgesia/anesthesia, antisepsis and obstetric guidance, support the fundus externally and follow the natural placental separation plane with the internal hand. Do not forcibly strip tissue if no separation plane is found; suspect placenta accreta and proceed to operative rescue. Give antibiotic prophylaxis for manual removal per protocol.

Perineal, vaginal or cervical trauma

Use good light, analgesia and adequate exposure; apply direct pressure to visible bleeding while resuscitating. Examine the vagina/cervix when bleeding persists despite a firm uterus. Assess the anal sphincter and rectal mucosa when a significant tear is suspected, with consent and appropriate examination. Repair uncomplicated first/second-degree tears only within competence and with full visualization of the apex. Third/fourth-degree tears, deep/expanding hematoma or uncontrolled cervical bleeding need specialist repair. Avoid blind clamping or deep sutures into an unseen apex.

Eclampsia: seizures and magnesium

Stabilize the mother; delivery follows stabilization

Seizure in pregnancy or postpartum: consider eclampsia even without severe hypertension/proteinuria, especially after 20 weeks or within 6 weeks postpartum. Treat the seizure and evaluate alternate causes.
  1. Protect airway and oxygenation. Call OB/anesthesia, position left lateral when feasible, prevent injury, suction as needed, check glucose and obtain IV access. Give oxygen for hypoxemia. Intubate for persistent loss of airway protection, hypoventilation or refractory seizures; do not restrain or put objects in the mouth.
  2. Magnesium sulfate: 4-6 g IV over 20-30 min by pump, then 1-2 g/h. Recurrent seizure: an additional 2 g IV over 5-10 min is commonly used. Persistent seizures require benzodiazepine/status management, airway support and evaluation for another cause. Heart block or myasthenia gravis: avoid magnesium and obtain an urgent alternative anticonvulsant plan.
  3. Magnesium safety: use a pump/dedicated line; monitor respiration, SpO2, reflexes and urine output. Adjust maintenance for renal impairment/oliguria. Stop and reassess for respiratory depression or absent reflexes. Suspected toxicity: stop magnesium, support ventilation and give calcium gluconate 1 g IV (10 mL of 10%) over 5 min, with monitoring. When toxicity is absent and magnesium remains indicated, continue the prescribed infusion during transfer; birth alone is not a reason to stop.
  4. Treat persistent severe BP: SBP at least 160 OR DBP at least 110 mmHg, persisting for about 15 min, requires prompt antihypertensive therapy, ideally within 30-60 min of confirmation. Do not delay treatment in an unstable patient. Magnesium prevents/treats seizures; it is not the BP drug.
  5. Reassess and arrange definitive care. Obtain CBC/platelets, renal and liver tests, urine protein as useful; assess for HELLP, pulmonary edema, stroke and abruption. Avoid indiscriminate fluid loading. Arrange urgent obstetric admission/transfer and delivery planning after maternal stabilization.
Treat persistent severe BP simultaneously using the Severe hypertension card. Magnesium is not an antihypertensive.

Maternal arrest: resuscitative delivery

Fundus at/above umbilicus + arrest not rapidly reversed

Prepare at recognition of arrest. Goal: complete delivery by 5 minutes if no ROSC. Do not wait until minute 4 to gather equipment. Perform at the arrest location; do not move an in-hospital arrest to the OR for this procedure.

Continue maternal resuscitation throughout

  • Standard high-quality adult CPR/ACLS, usual defibrillation energy and drugs. Keep her supine for effective compressions while a separate person manually displaces the uterus leftward. Prioritize oxygenation/airway; use IV/IO access above the diaphragm when feasible.
  • Call OB/surgery/anesthesia, neonatal support and blood bank. Assign a separate baby resuscitation lead. Remove fetal monitors; fetal-heart assessment/viability testing must not delay a maternal resuscitative procedure. Consider hemorrhage, embolism, eclampsia, cardiomyopathy, anesthetic complications and standard reversible causes. If arrest occurs during IV magnesium, stop it and consider IV calcium; do not wait for a magnesium level.
  • Fundus at/above the umbilicus is the practical trigger for clinically important aortocaval compression (often about 20 weeks or more). The procedure is for maternal resuscitation, regardless of fetal viability. If arrest has already been prolonged/unknown duration, do not restart a fresh waiting period.

Emergency hysterotomy sequence for a trained operator

  1. Gather scalpel, heavy/blunt-tipped scissors, cord clamps and large packs. Use PPE and rapid antisepsis if immediately available. Continue CPR, ventilation and hemorrhage support; do not wait for a full operative tray or sterile setup.
  2. Open the abdomen in the midline. Make a generous vertical incision with enough exposure to reach the gravid uterus; extend as required. Open fascia and peritoneum carefully, protecting bowel, and expose the uterus.
  3. Open the uterus. Identify and protect the bladder inferiorly. Make a controlled small midline uterine incision, avoiding fetal injury; extend vertically with scissors while protecting the fetus underneath with fingers/hand. Open membranes if intact.
  4. Deliver. Extract the fetus through the incision; double-clamp and cut the cord and hand the baby to the neonatal team. Check for another fetus. Deliver a separated placenta and address bleeding; do not forcibly strip an adherent placenta.
  5. Continue resuscitation and damage control. Uterine/abdominal hemorrhage control, packs and surgical assistance are needed immediately, especially with ROSC. Arrange operative repair and ICU care, along with neonatal disposition. If consciousness returns, provide anesthesia/analgesia as soon as feasible.
No on-site OB does not remove the indication. Establish who will perform this rescue and practice the sequence before covering an isolated ED. Maternal transfer before necessary resuscitative delivery can lose critical time. If rapid vaginal birth is already achievable, it may provide the fastest uterine evacuation.

Document arrest recognition, preparation, incision, birth and ROSC times. Failure to reach the 5-minute goal is not itself a reason to withhold delivery or stop ongoing resuscitation.

Adult tube thoracostomy

Open technique | Tension physiology: decompress without waiting for imaging

Correct side, true rib space, confirmed pleural entry. Use the 4th/5th intercostal space in the triangle of safety, usually just anterior to the midaxillary line. Nipple position/inframammary folds are unreliable landmarks.
  1. Decide the urgency and drain. Suspected tension with instability: immediate decompression; do not wait for CXR/ultrasound. Finger/open thoracostomy is appropriate when trained and immediately available, especially in traumatic arrest; needle decompression is a bridge when open access cannot be performed promptly. Bilateral decompression is part of a traumatic-arrest protocol when thoracic injury is possible, not a blanket step for every peri-arrest patient.
  2. Choose size for physiology. Unstable traumatic hemothorax/large air leak: a large-bore trauma drain, commonly 28-32 Fr (local protocol may use larger). A 14 Fr pigtail is an option in selected stable trauma. Under PPV, favor drainage; observation of an occult pneumothorax requires trauma-team selection, serial reassessment and immediate decompression capability. Do not assume these conditions exist during transfer.
  3. Position, check and mark. If physiology allows: head elevated, ipsilateral arm supported above/behind the head. Confirm side against examination and imaging with another clinician. Count ribs; identify the lateral pectoralis major and anterior latissimus borders. Ultrasound for pleural fluid is essential; use it to identify diaphragm/viscera and a safe window. Do not rely on absent lung sliding alone to diagnose pneumothorax.
  4. Analgesia and preparation. Sterile prep/drape. Infiltrate lidocaine from skin through periosteum and parietal pleura, aspirating before injection and allowing effect when possible. Provide titrated systemic analgesia/sedation with monitoring and airway support. In an immediately life-threatening collapse, do not delay decompression for full anesthesia.
  5. Incise and dissect. Make a 2-3 cm incision over the rib below the intended interspace. Bluntly dissect just over that rib’s superior border with a clamp, using controlled entry through pleura and spreading to make a tract. Avoid a thrusting trocar or uncontrolled force.
  6. Confirm with a finger. Place a gloved finger through the tract to confirm pleural entry and gently assess adjacent space. Do not blindly tear adhesions or force a circumferential sweep; adherent lung can be injured. If the expected space is not identifiable or solid organ is encountered, stop and reassess urgently.
  7. Insert without force. Guide the tube through the tract along the finger so every side hole is intrapleural. Direct toward the intended air/fluid space when practical, but function and hole position matter more than a perfect tip direction. Do not push hard against resistance.
  8. Connect, secure and verify. Connect immediately to a correctly assembled underwater-seal/drain system; document output/air leak. Secure with an anchoring suture and occlusive dressing; keep the canister upright below the chest. Prescribe water seal or suction (often -20 cm H2O when indicated) rather than assuming suction is always needed. Obtain/review CXR and reassess physiology after insertion.
Local anesthetic: 1% lidocaine = 10 mg/mL. Adult label maximum without epinephrine: 4.5 mg/kg, capped at 300 mg; with epinephrine: 7 mg/kg, capped at 500 mg. Use lower doses when indicated by illness, shock or other local anesthetics. For traumatic tubes, give antibiotic prophylaxis at insertion per protocol; cefazolin is a common choice. Do not delay life-saving drainage for it.

Adult Seldinger pigtail

Selected stable patients | Device-specific steps still apply

Seldinger pigtail: selected stable patients

  1. Choose and verify the site using the same side check, rib counting, asepsis and analgesia principles. Use ultrasound for fluid; consider image-guided placement for loculation/adhesions.
  2. Needle into pleural space, over the upper rib border. Aspirate the expected air/fluid. Pass the guidewire gently; maintain control of its external end throughout. Stop for resistance; do not force or withdraw a wire against a sharp needle edge.

Adult emergency cricothyrotomy

Cannot intubate, cannot oxygenate | Scalpel-finger-bougie-tube

Declare CICO and proceed to emergency front-of-neck access. Failed optimized mask/SGA oxygenation with failed intubation is the trigger; do not wait for profound desaturation or repeated laryngoscopy. Failed intubation alone with adequate oxygenation is not CICO.

Prepare while another clinician continues oxygenation

Call anesthesia/ENT/surgery; assign roles and continue upper-airway oxygen. Extend the neck as feasible; lifesaving oxygenation takes priority over spinal precautions. Ensure anesthesia and neuromuscular blockade rapidly; CICO does not imply unconsciousness. Do not delay rescue for drugs or a specialist.

Kit: #10 scalpel, standard adult bougie, lubricated 6.0-mm cuffed ETT, cuff syringe, bag/circuit, suction and waveform capnography. Do not cut down or modify the bougie; use the trained technique and standard equipment.

  1. Stabilize the larynx. Use a laryngeal handshake to identify thyroid cartilage, cricothyroid region and cricoid, keeping the larynx centered.
  2. Vertical skin incision. DAS 2025 recommends this as the default eFONA approach because surface identification can be unreliable. Make an adequate midline incision (commonly 8-10 cm in a difficult/impalpable neck), then bluntly dissect with a finger to identify the larynx and membrane. The midline can still bleed; maintain tactile orientation.
  3. Open the membrane transversely. Stabilize the larynx and make a controlled transverse stab through the cricothyroid membrane. Avoid plunging toward the posterior wall. Rotate the blade 90 degrees so the sharp edge points caudally, using it to hold the opening.
  4. Place the bougie. Slide the angled tip down the caudal side of the blade into the trachea, align toward the feet and advance gently about 10-15 cm. Never force it or deliberately seek distal “hold-up”; this can perforate the airway. Tracheal clicks may be absent and do not replace later confirmation.
  5. Railroad the tube. Remove the scalpel once the bougie is in the airway. Stabilize the tract and advance the lubricated 6.0 cuffed ETT over the bougie until the cuff is just fully inside the trachea; avoid adult oral-intubation depth. Remove the bougie, inflate the cuff and ventilate.
  6. Confirm and secure. Require a sustained waveform capnogram when circulation is present, plus chest expansion, bilateral air entry and improving oxygenation. Record depth at the skin, secure carefully, give ongoing analgesia/sedation and arrange specialist airway assessment.

Failure to ventilate

Check the circuit/obstruction, tube depth and cuff; suspect false passage with high resistance, absent chest movement and expanding neck emphysema. Stop insufflating a suspected false passage, re-identify the membrane with a finger and re-establish the tract. In arrest/very low perfusion, CO2 can be absent despite tracheal placement; use direct/tactile confirmation plus physiology and treat perfusion while checking position. Avoid repeatedly removing a correctly placed airway solely because of low-flow capnography.

Children: this is an adult card, not a universal pediatric rescue. Use the pediatric difficult-airway pathway and expert surgical support; age/anatomy, available devices and expertise determine access. A 14 G cannula plus improvised high-pressure oxygen is not a universally safe substitute, particularly without an expiratory route.

Afterward: check for mainstem placement, pneumothorax and injury; arrange ICU and ENT/surgery. Conversion to another airway is individualized, not automatically required within 24 hours. Document CICO, attempts, first effective ventilation and complications; debrief and arrange follow-up.
